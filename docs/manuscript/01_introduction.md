# Introduction

Metacognition, the process of monitoring and regulating our cognitive and perceptual processes, is crucial for optimal cognitive functioning and plays a fundamental role in our daily lives. Accurate self-evaluation is a critical step in developing effective cognitive strategies to enhance our strengths and overcome our weaknesses (Flavell, 1979). Investigating the biological and psycho-social factors that influence metacognitive ability is therefore essential for advancing our understanding of the human mind and its applications in education, the workplace and mental health. To address this need, this study investigates gender differences in metacognition across different domains and functional levels [using signal-detection models of metacognition]{.changed}.

Metacognition has gained increased research attention in recent years due to the development of [signal-detection]{.changed} models that allow for more detailed investigations into its determinants (Fleming et al., 2012; Fleming & Lau, 2014). These models delineate metacognitive ability into metacognitive bias and metacognitive efficiency (M-ratio), which respectively refer to our overall level of confidence and the sensitivity of our confidence to objective performance (Fleming, 2017; Maniscalco & Lau, 2012). [Bias in this sense is a statement about where confidence sits relative to a reference point, which is actual performance in the case of explicit performance estimates and the decision criterion in the case of trial-wise confidence criteria (O'Neill et al., 2026).]{.changed} Metacognition can be further divided into hierarchically local and global components (Rouault, McWilliams, et al., 2018; Seow et al., 2021), with global metacognition referring to the maintenance of explicit metacognitive self-beliefs and local metacognition corresponding to task-specific self-monitoring, as quantified by trial-wise confidence ratings.

This hierarchical approach has been used extensively to ask to what extent these aspects of metacognition are shared across cognitive domains. Several early studies found that metacognitive ability is correlated across perceptual and memory domains (Baird et al., 2013; McCurdy et al., 2013; for review, see Rouault, McWilliams, et al., 2018; Song et al., 2011). More recently, large-scale studies have confirmed that metacognitive bias and efficiency both exhibit a general shared factor (Lund et al., 2025; Mazancieux et al., 2020), [although the shared variance is modest and domain-specific components remain substantial (Lehmann, Hagen & Ettinger, 2022). Over-confidence in particular has long been known to have a domain-general component across knowledge and reasoning tasks (West & Stanovich, 1997).]{.changed} While these studies indicate that at a psychological level metacognitive ability is partly conserved across domains, studies using neural recordings have highlighted the unique contributions of domain-specific mechanisms (Morales et al., 2018). Consistent with this, the maintenance of global self-beliefs has recently been shown to be highly domain-specific (Hoogervorst et al., 2025).

Prior research indicates that metacognitive ability has both strong biological and sociocultural roots (Fleming et al., 2012; Friston & Frith, 2015; Heyes et al., 2020). Inter-individual differences in metacognitive ability have been linked to the structure and function of the prefrontal cortex (Allen et al., 2017; Fleming et al., 2010; Fleming & Dolan, 2012; McCurdy et al., 2013). Furthermore, metacognition differs across cultures and is theorised to have evolved specifically for the sociocultural transmission of higher-order beliefs and social alignment (Heyes et al., 2020; Shea et al., 2014). Metacognitive ability also continues to develop through adolescence (Weil et al., 2013). Considering the developmental and sociocultural trajectories of metacognitive development, it is reasonable to expect gender differences in metacognition.

Decades of research have investigated differences between men and women in self-esteem and self-confidence (Beyer, 1998; for reviews, see Gentile et al., 2009; Lirgg, 1991). A recurring finding is higher expressed confidence in men across domains such as mental rotation, arithmetic, and language (Cooke-Simpson & Voyer, 2007; Rivers et al., 2021; Stankov & Lee, 2008). [When confidence is compared with actual accuracy, the difference typically manifests as greater over-estimation in men rather than better calibration in either gender, in that men tend to over-estimate their performance more than women on general-knowledge and mathematics tests at matched accuracy (Bench et al., 2015; Lundeberg et al., 1994; Pallier, 2003). The same pattern also drives gender differences in economic decisions such as trading frequency and willingness to compete (Barber & Odean, 2001; Niederle & Vesterlund, 2007). Previous work has found that men's confidence is inflated for errors as well as for correct responses (Lundeberg et al., 1994), which points to a shift in confidence criteria rather than a difference in how well confidence discriminates correct from incorrect responses.]{.changed} These differences have consequences: in competitive settings such as job interviews, women are penalised for expressing confidence in ways that men are not (Kulich et al., 2011; Phelan et al., 2008). [Whether the gender difference extends to metacognitive efficiency is less clear. To address this, we conducted a large-scale study investigating both global and local metacognition across recognition memory, visual perception, and general knowledge about socioeconomics and nutrition. Participants completed a testing battery and answered questionnaires assessing their global metacognitive self-belief (explicit estimates of accuracy) on each task before and after completing the battery, which allowed us to assess self-beliefs both at baseline and in their updating following testing. From local confidence ratings and task performance we estimated metacognitive bias and metacognitive efficiency within a single hierarchical Bayesian signal-detection model in which gender could affect every model parameter. Bias was measured against a reference point at both levels: global calibration (self-estimated minus actual per cent correct) and meta-Δ (the placement of confidence criteria relative to the metacognitive decision criterion). We then repeated the general knowledge measures in an independent sample of 456 participants.

On the basis of the literature above we predicted a domain-general gender difference in metacognitive bias at both global and local levels, with men expressing more confidence than women at matched performance. For metacognitive efficiency the prior evidence is mixed and a recent analysis of perceptual datasets from the Confidence Database found no overall gender difference while leaving small effects unresolved (Xue et al., 2024), so we treated the presence and direction of a gender difference in efficiency as an open question, and we asked in addition whether any gender differences were shared across domains or specific to some of them.
