# Methods

## Participants

330 healthy Danish participants completed the study. All participants were above the age of 18, were fluent in English, and had normal or corrected-to-normal vision. A single participant was excluded from these analyses for not identifying as either female or male and 12 were excluded from the behavioural analyses: 11 for incomplete data and one (participant 214) as an outlier in the original preprocessing. After exclusion, 317 participants (172 women, 54.3%; 145 men, 45.7%) were included in the behavioural analyses. Participants were aged between 18 and 65; the average age was 23.8 years (SD = 4.06) for women and 24.8 years (SD = 6.28) for men. [Gender was self-reported by participants; race and ethnicity were not recorded. Participants were paid DKK 110 per hour for the session, which lasted about 1.5 hours.]{.changed} [The hierarchical meta-d′ model was fitted to the participant list prepared for the original HMeta-d analysis, which retained participant 214 (318 participants: 172 women, 146 men); one of his cells (GDP, d′ < 0) was removed by the cell-level quality control described below.]{.changed}

Ethical approval was granted by the [Cognition and Behavior Lab (COBELAB) Institutional Review Board (IRB)]{.changed} at Aarhus University, Denmark, and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013). [All participants gave informed consent before taking part.]{.changed} The data were collected as part of a pre-registered study of domain-generality of metacognition (https://osf.io/jm47b). [The preregistration covered data preprocessing and the domain-generality analyses reported by Lund et al. (2025) and Hoogervorst et al. ## Procedure

Participants were tested at the Cognition and Behavior (COBE) lab at Aarhus University, where a parallel testing facility was used. The testing room seated up to 16 participants simultaneously, with individual privacy screens ensuring confidentiality. The study began with a general overview of the experimental procedures provided by one of the experimenters, including instructions on the counterbalanced task order. The tasks were then demonstrated briefly, followed by a training session where participants could ask the experimenter questions. Before starting the main experiment, participants completed a demographics questionnaire and a prospective self-belief questionnaire (Supplementary Table S1) to provide additional information about their background and global metacognitive beliefs.

The main experiment comprised four distinct domains: recognition memory, perception, and [two general knowledge domains]{.changed} (Fig. 1). To minimise first-order confounds, a two-alternative forced choice design (2AFC) was employed for all tasks (Lee et al., 2018). Additionally, decision and confidence rating intervals were standardised in appearance and timing across all tasks to maximise similarity and facilitate the comparison of cross-domain metacognitive ability. After completing all four tasks, participants completed a retrospective self-belief questionnaire (Supplementary Table S1) and were asked to exit the testing room quietly to avoid disturbing others. The experiment took approximately 105 minutes to complete, and all participants were compensated 195 DKK in accordance with standard COBE lab rates.

## Task design

In this study, we tested four different domains of metacognition across three individual tasks. Specifically, we used a recognition memory task, a visual dot numerosity task, and a newly created [general knowledge task that spanned two domains (presented to participants as the "trivia" test)]{.changed}. The recognition memory task and visual dot numerosity task were exact replications of previous studies conducted by McCurdy et al. (2013) and Rouault, Seow, et al. (2018), respectively. [The general knowledge task]{.changed} was designed to test domain-general metacognition in specific categories of semantic memory (Lund et al., 2025). We provide full details of these tasks in their respective publications but briefly describe the stimuli and trial details of each task here.

To ensure a fair comparison of metacognitive ability across all domains, we standardised the presentation of all tasks. Specifically, we controlled for inter-stimulus and inter-response intervals, stimulus durations, and visual presentation. Furthermore, we implemented all tasks as 2AFC, as this response format has been shown to be optimal for estimating inter-individual differences (Lee et al., 2018). In each task, participants viewed two stimuli, made a discrimination judgement via button press, and then rated their subjective confidence in that judgement. [Confidence was rated on a discrete seven-point scale, shown as a horizontal line at the centre of the screen, above the fixation cross, with seven tick marks numbered 1 to 7 and the prompt "Confidence?" beneath it. The scale had no verbal anchors. A marker appeared at a randomly chosen tick on every trial, so that the starting position carried no information, and participants moved it one tick at a time with the left and right arrow keys and confirmed with the space bar. Ratings had to be confirmed within 4 s; trials without a confirmed rating in that time were recorded as missing (0.7% of trials) and excluded. The scale was identical in every domain. All analyses treat the ratings on their native seven-point scale.]{.changed} We instructed participants to respond as quickly and accurately as possible across all tasks.

The memory task involved memorising a list of 50 words in each block of trials. Since this task could not be staircased adaptively, difficulty was manipulated blockwise by giving participants 30, 60, or 90 seconds to memorise each list of words. Afterwards, participants were presented with all 50 words in random order, along with a non-memorised distractor word, and had to identify which of the two words they had previously seen.

In the vision task, participants were shown two circular clouds of dots and had to determine which one had a higher total number of dots. Performance was controlled by an adaptive staircase that updated the difference in dot totals across trials using a two-down one-up procedure, which converged at 71% accuracy at the limit.

[In the general knowledge task]{.changed}, participants had to make judgements about either the caloric content of different foods (Calories condition) or the GDP per capita of different countries (GDP condition). In the Calories condition, two plates of food were presented on the screen during each trial. Participants had to identify which of the two plates had a higher caloric content per 100 g. In the GDP condition, the flags of two countries were displayed on screen with the country names, and participants had to determine which of the two countries had the higher average GDP per capita between 2010 and 2017, as stated in the on-screen question. In both cases, performance was staircased using a two-down one-up procedure across trials, either based on the total difference in calories between the two plates of food or the log-GDP difference between the countries. Throughout the paper we refer to the two [general knowledge]{.changed} domains by their task names, GDP and Calories, alongside the memory and vision domains.

## Measurement of self-beliefs

To assess global metacognition and how participants updated their self-beliefs following the testing battery, we measured their explicit self-beliefs about their performance before and after the testing session using a self-report inventory. For each domain, participants were presented with a prompt describing the upcoming task, such as "During the memory test, you will be asked to memorise lists of words. Later, you will see each of these words again, together with a new distractor word. You will have to choose which of the two words you have seen before. There will be about 200 trials in total. Please estimate the total percent of trials you expect to answer correctly on the memory test (0-100%):" Participants were asked to provide their best guess on a scale from 0 to 100% accuracy using a numeric input response. The questions were identical to the retrospective questionnaire but were reworded to the past tense. Refer to Supplementary Table S1 for the full question texts.

## Replication sample

To test whether the gender effects on local confidence and on the model-based measures observed in the discovery sample are replicable, we analysed the same GDP and Calories general knowledge task from the Visceral Mind Project, a large-scale brain imaging and phenotyping study at the Centre of Functionally Integrative Neuroscience, Aarhus University (Banellis et al., 2026). Participants were recruited in two cohorts through national participant pools, social media, newspaper advertisements, and posted fliers. Participants were required to have normal or corrected-to-normal vision, fluency in Danish or English, and compatibility with MRI scanning, and were paid for their participation. The local Region Midtjylland Ethics Committee granted approval for the study, and all participants provided informed consent; the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The project involved multiple sessions of MRI, behavioural tasks, physiological recordings, and questionnaires; the general knowledge task was completed in one of the behavioural sessions, with each participant tested individually. The task code, staircase procedure, trial count (about 100 trials per domain), stimuli, and seven-point confidence scale were identical to the discovery sample. 458 participants completed both general knowledge domains; one who did not identify as a woman or a man was excluded, leaving 457 (289 women, 168 men; 189 from the first cohort and 268 from the second). [Gender was self-reported by participants; race and ethnicity were not recorded.]{.changed} Women were aged 24.3 years on average (SD = 5.0, range 18 to 56) and men 25.5 years (SD = 4.6, range 18 to 52). After cell-level quality control (see below), 456 participants entered the hierarchical model. The replication sample did not complete the memory or vision tasks or the self-belief questionnaires, and years of education were not recorded, so the replication contributes to the local confidence and hierarchical-model analyses only, with age as the sole covariate in the behavioural models (the hierarchical model has no covariates in either sample).

## Data analysis

::: changed
### Preprocessing

Data preprocessing followed the pre-registered pipeline. For the behavioural models, trials with a decision reaction time under 50 ms were removed, and trials whose decision or confidence reaction time lay more than 1.5 interquartile ranges beyond the quartiles of the pooled trial distribution (Tukey, 1977; in the replication sample, decision reaction time only) were excluded, as were trials without a confidence rating, resulting in the removal of 11% of trials. Participants who then lacked either one correct or one incorrect trial in any domain were excluded, as the accuracy-split models described below require both trial types. The hierarchical meta-d′ model, which does not use reaction times, was fitted to every trial with a response and a confidence rating. All analyses were run in R 4.4 (R Core Team, 2024) with brms (Bürkner, 2017) and Stan via cmdstanr (Carpenter et al., 2017); figures use ggplot2 (Wickham, 2016). Software versions are listed in Supplementary Table S11 and full model specifications in Supplementary Note 1.

### Statistical approach

All analyses were conducted within a Bayesian framework. All effects are reported as a posterior mean with its 95% highest-density interval (HDI), a Savage–Dickey Bayes factor against a point null of zero (BF10; Wagenmakers et al., 2010), and the percentage of the posterior inside a region of practical equivalence (ROPE; Kruschke, 2018). We describe BF10 above 3 as moderate and above 10 as strong evidence for an effect, and BF10 below 1/3 (BF01 above 3) as moderate evidence for the null; values between are described as inconclusive. We quote BF10 for effects and BF01 (= 1/BF10) for claims of no difference, so that the number reported is always the evidence for the claim being made (Keysers, Gazzola & Wagenmakers, 2020); the tables report BF10 throughout. Gender is sum-coded (+0.5 women, −0.5 men), so every gender coefficient represents the women − men contrast. Domain is cell-means coded (no intercept), so every gender effect is estimated within a domain. The domain-general effect is the draw-wise mean of the four per-domain effects rather than a separate coefficient, and domain differences are the pairwise differences between per-domain effects, each with its own HDI; the behavioural models also give each a Bayes factor, whereas for the hierarchical model we report Bayes factors for the per-domain and domain-general contrasts only. The same gender estimands are used in every model, rendering the behavioural and model-based results directly comparable. Bayes factors were computed via the Savage–Dickey method from the prior and posterior draws of the coefficient, or of the draw-wise mean or difference for domain-general and pairwise effects. Priors for all models are listed in Supplementary Table S3, and every main result was re-estimated with narrower and wider contrast priors: half and double the width in the behavioural models, and SD 0.2 and 1.0 in place of 0.5 in the hierarchical model (Supplementary Table S7).

### Behavioural models

The analyses of task performance, global calibration, local confidence, reaction time, staircase variability and the local–global link all employ Gaussian multilevel regression models fitted with brms, with the identity link for the mean and a log link for the residual standard deviation. The unit of analysis is the participant × domain cell, split further by time for calibration and by trial accuracy for confidence and reaction time, and the outcome is the cell mean. The mean has a cell-means design: a coefficient for each domain, every contrast (gender, time, trial accuracy and their interactions) nested within domain, standardised age and years of education as covariates, and a participant random intercept. The residual standard deviation is estimated separately for each within-participant design cell (domain × time or domain × accuracy), because between-participant spread differs several-fold across domains and a pooled residual would misstate the precision of every per-domain contrast. [Data distribution was assumed to be normal but this was not formally tested; participant-level data are shown alongside the summary statistics in Figures 2 and 3 and Supplementary Figures S1–S3.]{.changed} Each model was sampled with 4 chains of 4,000 iterations (1,000 warm-up) at adapt_delta 0.95, with prior draws stored for the Savage–Dickey Bayes factors. Equations for each model are given in Supplementary Note 1.

### Task performance

Gender differences in accuracy and mean reaction time per domain were estimated with the behavioural model described below, with one observation per participant and domain. These serve as the positive control that task difficulty was matched between genders.

### Global calibration

Global bias is measured as calibration, the participant's self-estimated per cent correct minus their actual per cent correct in a given domain. A calibration value of zero indicates a well-calibrated self-estimate, negative values indicate under-estimation and positive values indicate over-estimation. Calibration was modelled as a function of gender, time (pre- versus post-task, sum-coded) and domain, with age and years of education as standardised covariates, a participant random intercept and a residual standard deviation free to vary over the within-participant design cells. The gender × time interaction tests whether the pre-to-post update in self-belief differs by gender.

### Local confidence

Mean confidence on the seven-point scale is reported as a descriptive quantity; it is not a bias in the normative sense because the scale has no reference point. It was modelled as a function of gender, trial accuracy (correct versus incorrect, sum-coded) and domain, with each participant's overall accuracy in that domain (z-scored within domain) as a covariate to estimate gender effects on confidence at matched accuracy, plus age and education covariates and a participant random intercept. The gender × accuracy interaction tests whether the gender difference in confidence differs between correct and incorrect trials. The same model, with two domains and without the education covariate, was fitted to the replication sample.

### Hierarchical model of metacognitive efficiency and bias

Metacognitive efficiency (M-ratio, meta-d′/d′) and metacognitive bias (meta-Δ) were estimated in a single hierarchical Bayesian signal-detection model fitted to all participants, trials, and domains at once, using the hmetad family for brms (an implementation of the HMeta-d model of Fleming, 2017, in Stan; O'Neill et al., 2026). The data for each participant × domain cell are the trial counts over the 14 joint response × confidence categories for each stimulus class, modelled with a multinomial likelihood whose probabilities are derived from the meta-d′ signal-detection model with Gaussian noise (Maniscalco & Lau, 2012; Fleming, 2017). The model consists of 15 distributional parameters per participant and domain: log M-ratio (log link), d′ and the type-1 criterion c (identity link), and twelve log-spacings (log link) that place the six confidence criteria on each response side at successive distances from the metacognitive decision criterion meta-c. Every parameter was given the same linear predictor, a domain cell mean plus a within-domain gender contrast, with participant-level random effects for each domain that are correlated across domains, with an LKJ(2) prior on the correlation matrix. Allowing gender to affect every parameter matters for the interpretation of log M-ratio. If the confidence criteria were constrained to be equal across genders, any gender difference in how the confidence scale is used would be absorbed by log M-ratio and would appear as a spurious difference in efficiency. The between-domain correlations of the participant-level log M-ratio effects were included as model parameters and provide the domain-generality estimate.

Meta-Δ (O'Neill et al., 2026) represents the mean distance, on the internal evidence axis, between the six confidence criteria and the metacognitive decision criterion meta-c, computed from the fitted criterion spacings on each response side and averaged over the two sides (Supplementary Note 1). A larger meta-Δ indicates that a participant requires more evidence before reporting a given level of confidence. Meta-Δ is defined net of d′, c and meta-d′, and therefore separates the placement of confidence criteria from metacognitive sensitivity. A positive women − men contrast signifies that women place their confidence criteria more conservatively. Meta-Δ is zero only for a participant who uses nothing but the highest confidence rating, so its absolute value has no normative reference (O'Neill et al., 2026); we interpret only differences between groups and domains.

The confidence criteria were anchored at meta-c = c (the HMeta-d default, referred to as the fixed-criterion model here); a variant with meta-c/meta-d′ = c/d′, so that the criteria move relative to the type-1 criterion, gave the same results and is reported in Supplementary Table S10 and Figure S4. Participant × domain cells with d′ ≤ 0 or fewer than 30 trials were excluded before fitting, because M-ratio is undefined when d′ ≤ 0 and poorly identified from very few trials (2 of 1,272 cells in the discovery sample, 7 of 914 in the replication; Supplementary Table S2). The model was sampled with 4 chains of 2,000 iterations (1,000 warm-up), adapt_delta 0.95 and maximum tree depth 12. Convergence was assessed on the reported coefficients (R-hat and effective sample size; Supplementary Table S9); prior draws were stored for the Savage–Dickey Bayes factors. Posterior predictive checks compared observed and model-predicted accuracy and mean confidence in every gender × domain cell (Supplementary Figures S6 and S7 and Table S9). The region of practical equivalence for log M-ratio was ±0.05 log units, i.e., approximately a 5% difference in M-ratio.

M-ratio can be biased when stimulus difficulty varies within a task (Rahnev & Fleming, 2019), as it does under a staircase. Since the gender contrast is estimated within domain, both genders faced the same staircase rule. To probe this potential bias further, we additionally tested whether the amount of difficulty variation differed by gender (see below).

### Confound checks

Three further multilevel models addressed specific alternative explanations. Reaction time was modelled by gender, trial accuracy, and domain, with the gender × accuracy interaction to examine whether errors by men are selectively fast, which would inflate their apparent metacognitive sensitivity. Staircase variability, i.e., the within-participant standard deviation of the absolute stimulus level (z-scored within domain) in the three staircased domains, was modelled by gender and domain. Finally, the link between local and global metacognition was investigated by regressing each participant's log M-ratio and meta-Δ (posterior means from the hierarchical model) on their pre-test calibration and on their pre-to-post update, both z-scored within domain, with gender as a moderator. These regressions are descriptive: they use participant-level posterior means, which are shrunk toward the group mean, and do not propagate the uncertainty in those estimates.

### Priors

In the behavioural models, cell means and standardised covariates had normal(0, s) priors with s the standard deviation of the outcome, every contrast coefficient (anything carrying gender, time or accuracy) had a normal(0, 0.5 s) prior, random-effect standard deviations had half-normal(0, s) priors, and the log residual standard deviations normal(log s, 1); the ROPE was ±0.1 s. Prior sensitivity used contrast widths of 0.25 s and s. In the hierarchical model the domain means had normal(0, 0.5) priors on log M-ratio and c, normal(1, 0.5) on d′ and normal(−0.5, 1) on the criterion log-spacings; the gender contrasts had normal(0, 0.5) priors on every parameter, with sensitivity refits at 0.2 and 1.0; random-effect standard deviations had half-normal(0, 1) priors (Supplementary Tables S3 and S7).
