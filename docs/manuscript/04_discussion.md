Women reported lower confidence than men after accounting for task performance, and signal-detection modelling identified more conservative confidence criteria in both samples. In the discovery sample, women also underestimated their overall performance more strongly and revised their self-beliefs further downward after testing. These differences were not accompanied by a robust gender difference in metacognitive efficiency. The findings suggest that gender differences in confidence involve both the standards used to evaluate individual decisions and broader beliefs about ability.

## Global self-beliefs

Both women and men underestimated their performance, but the underestimation was greater in women and increased more after testing. By the end of the session, women estimated their accuracy about twenty percentage points below their actual performance, compared with twelve points for men. Because both groups had already underestimated their performance before testing, these downward revisions made their self-estimates less accurate and widened the gender gap. This greater underestimation occurred without evidence of poorer monitoring of individual decisions in women.

Previous work suggests that estimates of overall performance draw on both confidence in individual decisions and prior beliefs about ability (Rouault et al., 2019; Seow et al., 2021). More negative prior beliefs offer one possible explanation for the persistent underestimation observed here. However, the present analyses do not establish why women revised their estimates more negatively after testing.

## Local confidence

Women expressed lower confidence on both correct and incorrect trials after accounting for overall accuracy. The model attributed this pattern to more conservative confidence criteria, a finding reproduced in the independent general knowledge sample. The results provided no consistent evidence that the confidence gap was specific to errors. This pattern agrees with earlier reports of higher confidence in men for both correct and incorrect answers (Lundeberg et al., 1994), and extends work showing gender differences in confidence at matched performance (Bench et al., 2015; Pallier, 2003).

A higher confidence threshold does not by itself show that confidence is inappropriately low. Meta-Δ describes where confidence criteria fall within the model; it does not define an optimal level of confidence. The estimates of overall performance provide a more direct measure of calibration: both groups underestimated their accuracy, and women did so more strongly. More cautious expressions of confidence could nevertheless be adaptive in settings where errors or expressions of certainty carry greater social costs. Reports that women face more negative evaluation for confidence and errors make social experience a plausible influence on their confidence criteria (Carlin et al., 2018; Connor & Fiske, 2018; Fisk, 2018; Herbst, 2020). The present study cannot determine whether this explains the observed gender difference.

## Metacognitive efficiency

Evidence for a gender difference in metacognitive efficiency was less consistent than the evidence for a difference in confidence criteria. The discovery sample suggested a small advantage for women, about 8% in M-ratio, whereas the replication estimate was smaller, about 4%, and uncertain. These findings do not establish a reproducible efficiency difference, but they also do not exclude a small one. This interpretation is consistent with the meta-analysis of Xue et al. (2024), which found no overall gender difference while leaving small effects unresolved. More trials per participant and larger samples would help estimate such differences more precisely. Models that also account for decision time may provide a useful extension (Desender et al., 2022).

Additional analyses found no clear gender difference in error-related slowing or in the variability of task difficulty, which has been shown to bias M-ratio (Rahnev & Fleming, 2019); either could otherwise complicate the interpretation of metacognitive efficiency. Allowing confidence criteria to vary by gender also helped separate differences in confidence reporting from differences in efficiency.

## Domain generality

The gender difference in confidence was largest for socioeconomic knowledge and smaller for nutritional knowledge, with the same contrast evident in both samples. Memory and perception showed intermediate differences in the discovery sample. The two general knowledge domains used the same response format, staircase procedure and confidence scale, making their contrasting results informative about the role of task content. One possible explanation is that expectations about competence differ across domains, as suggested by previous work on gender and confidence (Bench et al., 2015; Lundeberg et al., 1994). We did not measure such expectations or participants' familiarity with the content, however, so their contribution remains uncertain. The findings support considering the domain when interpreting gender differences in confidence.

## Local and global metacognition

Participants with higher initial self-estimates tended to have greater metacognitive efficiency and less conservative confidence criteria. Larger downward revisions of self-beliefs were associated with lower efficiency and more conservative criteria. These associations are consistent with previous work linking confidence in individual decisions to broader beliefs about performance (Rouault et al., 2019; Hoogervorst et al., 2025). We found no consistent evidence that the associations differed by gender, although some moderation estimates remained inconclusive. The results therefore leave open whether the processes that connect local confidence to global self-beliefs differ between women and men.

## Limitations

Both samples consisted predominantly of young adults recruited in Denmark, which limits generalisation to other age groups and cultural settings. Field of study, socioeconomic background and exposure to gender stereotypes were not recorded, and may be relevant to the differences between tasks. The analyses were restricted to participants who identified as women or men; too few participants reported another gender to support a separate analysis.

Participants in the discovery sample were tested in groups of up to 16 in a shared room, separated by privacy screens. Social context can influence self-evaluation (Fastame & Penna, 2012), and women and men may respond differently to being tested alongside others, so the testing setting may have contributed to the gender difference in reported confidence. The replication sample, in which each participant was tested individually, reproduced the gender difference in confidence criteria, which makes it unlikely that the group setting alone accounts for it. The associations between local and global measures were estimated from participant-level posterior means and do not carry the uncertainty in those estimates. The observational design also leaves open how these differences in confidence develop and whether they reflect prior social experience.

## Conclusion

Women expressed lower confidence at matched accuracy, with more conservative confidence criteria in both samples. In the discovery sample, they also held more negative beliefs about overall performance. These findings suggest that the gender confidence gap concerns confidence thresholds and self-beliefs, without evidence of poorer monitoring of individual decisions.
