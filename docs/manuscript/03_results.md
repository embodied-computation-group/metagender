## Task performance

Task difficulty was matched between genders. Accuracy did not differ credibly in any domain: the domain-general difference was +0.6 percentage points (pp) [0.0, 1.2] (BF10 = 1.3), with the largest per-domain differences in memory (+1.8 pp [−0.1, 3.6], BF10 = 1.8) and Calories (+1.1 pp [0.1, 2.2], BF10 = 1.7), both inconclusive, and evidence for no difference in vision (BF01 = 14) and GDP (BF01 = 3.4). In the hierarchical model, which estimates d′ per gender and domain directly, the domain-general d′ contrast was +0.035 [−0.001, 0.074] in the discovery sample (BF01 = 2.3) and −0.005 [−0.041, 0.029] in the replication (BF01 = 18). Mean reaction time did not differ overall (+0.02 s [−0.03, 0.07], BF01 = 3.4); women were slower in GDP by 0.09 s [0.02, 0.15] (BF10 = 4.6) and did not differ elsewhere (Supplementary Figure S5 and Table S12). Because the memory and Calories accuracy differences favour women, all confidence analyses below control for accuracy.

## Global calibration

We found that participants under-estimated their own accuracy in every domain, before and after testing (Figure 2, Table 1). Averaged over domains, men estimated their accuracy 6.3 pp [4.5, 8.1] below their actual accuracy before testing and 11.7 pp [9.9, 13.6] below it afterwards; women were at 11.0 pp [9.3, 12.7] below before and 20.2 pp [18.4, 21.8] below afterwards. Neither gender over-estimated in any domain at either time point.

Women were less well calibrated than men by 6.6 pp [4.4, 8.8] overall (BF10 > 10¹⁵). The gap was largest in GDP (−15.0 pp [−18.0, −12.1], BF10 > 10¹⁵), present in vision (−5.1 pp [−7.9, −2.3], BF10 = 39) and Calories (−3.7 pp [−6.8, −0.9], BF10 = 3.8), and inconclusive in memory (−2.6 pp [−5.5, 0.3], BF10 = 0.7). GDP differed from every other domain (all BF10 > 10¹⁵); memory, vision, and Calories did not differ from one another (all BF01 > 2.3).

Global self-beliefs became more negative after testing across both genders (−7.4 pp [−8.5, −6.3], BF10 > 10²⁰), and more so in women, with a gender × time interaction of −3.8 pp [−5.9, −1.6] (BF10 = 135). Per domain the interaction was −5.5 pp in GDP (BF10 = 5.4), −4.8 pp in vision (BF10 = 3.6), −3.5 pp in Calories (BF10 = 0.9) and −1.2 pp in memory (BF10 = 0.3). The larger downward update in women therefore moved them further from calibration rather than toward it.

## Local confidence

After controlling for participant-level accuracy, women reported lower confidence than men, by 0.27 scale points [0.14, 0.41] overall (BF10 = 1.6 × 10³; Figure 3, Table 2). As with calibration, the difference was domain-graded, in the same direction in every domain but of very different sizes: −0.62 [−0.79, −0.45] in GDP (BF10 > 10¹⁵), −0.22 [−0.38, −0.06] in memory (BF10 = 5.3), −0.21 [−0.37, −0.06] in vision (BF10 = 4.5), and −0.04 [−0.18, 0.12] in Calories (BF01 = 7.4, 90% of the posterior in the ROPE). GDP differed from every other domain (all BF10 > 10¹⁵), memory and vision did not differ (BF01 = 12), and the difference between Calories and the memory and vision domains was inconclusive (BF10 = 2.8 and 1.9; Supplementary Table S4).

Confidence was higher on correct compared to incorrect trials in every domain (+1.22 [1.17, 1.27] overall). Reduced confidence in women was observed on correct trials (−0.21 [−0.36, −0.08], BF10 = 16) as well as on incorrect trials (−0.33 [−0.48, −0.19], BF10 = 8 × 10¹⁰). The gender × accuracy interaction, which would indicate that women lower their confidence selectively after errors, was +0.12 [0.02, 0.22] with BF10 = 1.9, and this inconclusive value was carried by the memory domain (+0.25 [0.04, 0.45], BF10 = 2.7); in the other three domains the interaction was between +0.06 and +0.10 with BF01 between 4 and 5.

## Metacognitive efficiency and bias

The hierarchical model converged (R-hat ≤ 1.02 on all reported coefficients, ≤ 1.002 on the log M-ratio coefficients, bulk effective sample size above 1,700 for every log M-ratio coefficient) and reproduced the data: observed accuracy fell inside the 95% posterior predictive interval in every gender × domain cell, and predicted mean confidence was within 0.03 scale points of the observed value in every cell, although the observed value lay outside the 95% predictive interval in 5 of the 12 cells (Supplementary Table S9 and Figures S6 and S7).

**Domain effects.** Metacognitive efficiency differed between domains. Log M-ratio was highest in GDP (0.20 [0.14, 0.25]) and memory (0.17 [0.14, 0.20]), which did not differ (−0.03 [−0.09, 0.03]), and lower in vision (−0.22 [−0.27, −0.16]) and Calories (−0.34 [−0.43, −0.25]); all other pairwise differences excluded zero (Supplementary Table S5). The participant-level correlations of log M-ratio between domains were small (0.04 to 0.22) and every 95% HDI included zero (Supplementary Table S6), so individual differences in efficiency were largely domain-specific in this sample.

**Gender and efficiency.** The domain-general gender contrast on log M-ratio was +0.078 [0.022, 0.139] (BF10 = 3.8; 17% of the posterior inside the ±0.05 ROPE), corresponding to an M-ratio about 8% higher in women (Figure 4, Table 3). No single domain excluded zero: memory +0.012 [−0.045, 0.071] (BF01 = 16, 88% in ROPE), vision +0.091 [−0.009, 0.203] (BF10 = 0.4), GDP +0.086 [−0.022, 0.192] (BF10 = 0.4), Calories +0.124 [−0.025, 0.284] (BF10 = 0.6). The six pairwise gender × domain differences all included zero (largest: memory − Calories, −0.11 [−0.28, 0.04]; Supplementary Table S5). The evidence for a gender difference in efficiency in this sample is therefore moderate for a small domain-general effect and absent for any domain-specific one.

**Gender and bias.** Meta-Δ was larger in women than in men. The domain-general contrast was +0.086 [0.064, 0.107] (BF10 = 1.1 × 10⁶). As for calibration and mean confidence, the effect was graded: GDP +0.194 [0.161, 0.228] (BF10 > 10¹¹), vision +0.081 [0.049, 0.114] (BF10 = 240), memory +0.055 [0.020, 0.089] (BF10 = 2.5, inconclusive although the HDI excludes zero) and Calories +0.014 [−0.004, 0.032] (BF01 = 25). The GDP contrast exceeded every other domain's, with non-overlapping HDIs, and the Calories contrast was the smallest (Table 3).

Gender effects on the type-1 parameters were small: d′ was higher in women in memory (+0.14 [0.02, 0.25], BF10 = 1.6) and did not differ elsewhere; the type-1 criterion did not differ except in vision (+0.07 [0.01, 0.14], BF10 = 0.6). Because these parameters are estimated jointly with log M-ratio and meta-Δ, the bias and efficiency contrasts above are already net of them.

## Replication

In the replication sample the gender effect on confidence was reproduced. With accuracy controlled, women reported lower confidence by −0.31 [−0.44, −0.18] (BF10 = 9.6 × 10⁶), −0.48 [−0.63, −0.33] in GDP (BF10 > 10¹⁵) and −0.14 [−0.27, −0.01] in Calories (BF10 = 1.0), with the GDP − Calories difference again decisive (BF10 > 10¹⁵; Supplementary Figure S3). The gender × accuracy interaction was +0.04 [−0.08, 0.16] (BF01 = 5.6, 94% in the ROPE): the gender difference was the same size on correct (−0.29 [−0.42, −0.15]) and incorrect trials (−0.33 [−0.48, −0.18]). Together with the discovery sample, this does not support a selective gender difference in sensitivity to errors.

The hierarchical model gave the same meta-Δ contrast as in the discovery sample: +0.086 [0.067, 0.106] overall (BF10 = 3.7 × 10⁶), +0.138 [0.107, 0.166] in GDP (BF10 > 10⁶) and +0.033 [0.017, 0.049] in Calories (BF10 = 17; Figure 4, Table 3). The efficiency contrast was in the same direction but smaller and imprecise: log M-ratio was +0.041 [−0.041, 0.124] overall (BF01 = 5.3 against a point null, but 43% of the posterior outside the ±0.05 ROPE), +0.041 [−0.054, 0.133] in GDP and +0.041 [−0.084, 0.173] in Calories, with no gender × domain difference (0.00 [−0.15, 0.16]). Type-1 sensitivity was matched (d′ contrast −0.005 [−0.041, 0.029]). Domain effects replicated: log M-ratio was higher in GDP than Calories by 0.50 [0.42, 0.59] (discovery sample: 0.54 [0.43, 0.63]), and the between-domain correlation was 0.11 [−0.06, 0.29].

## Alternative explanations

**Reaction times.** If men made more fast guesses, their errors would be selectively fast and their apparent metacognitive sensitivity inflated. Participants slowed on error trials in every domain (+0.22 s [0.20, 0.24] overall), and this error slowing did not differ by gender: +0.02 s [−0.02, 0.05] (BF01 = 4.6, 98% in the ROPE), with BF01 between 3.8 and 9 in every domain (Supplementary Figure S5A–B).

**Staircase variability.** M-ratio is sensitive to within-task variation in difficulty (Rahnev & Fleming, 2019). The within-participant standard deviation of the stimulus level did not differ by gender: +0.03 SD [−0.10, 0.16] overall (BF01 = 4.0, 87% in the ROPE), −0.04 in vision (BF01 = 4.1), −0.09 in Calories (BF01 = 3.3) and +0.22 [0.01, 0.43] in GDP (BF10 = 1.6; Supplementary Figure S5C–D). Difficulty variability therefore did not differ by gender overall; the GDP difference was inconclusive, and GDP is not a domain with a credible gender difference in efficiency. The check rests on one summary of difficulty variation and does not rule out every effect of trial-to-trial variation in difficulty on M-ratio.

**Covariates.** Age and years of education were covariates in the behavioural models of the discovery sample, and age in those of the replication; the hierarchical meta-d′ model did not include covariates. Neither covariate had a credible effect on confidence or, in the local–global regressions, on log M-ratio or meta-Δ (all BF01 > 10). We did not refit the models without covariates.

## Link between local and global metacognition

Local and global measures were coupled. Participants who rated themselves higher before testing had higher log M-ratio (+0.04 [0.02, 0.06] per SD of calibration, BF10 > 10⁹) and smaller meta-Δ (−0.10 [−0.12, −0.08] per SD, BF10 > 10¹⁵), and participants whose self-beliefs fell more after testing had smaller log M-ratio and larger meta-Δ (Supplementary Table S8). Gender moderation of the coupling was weak or absent: the gender × calibration term was +0.01 [−0.02, 0.04] for log M-ratio (BF01 = 4.5) and −0.02 [−0.06, 0.01] for meta-Δ (BF01 = 2.1), and the gender × update term was +0.01 [−0.02, 0.04] for log M-ratio (BF01 = 4.7) and −0.04 [−0.07, −0.00] for meta-Δ (BF10 = 2.3, inconclusive, carried by GDP). We therefore found no consistent evidence that the relation between how people judge single decisions and how they judge whole tasks differs between women and men.

## Prior sensitivity

The main conclusions did not depend on the prior width, but several secondary results did (Supplementary Table S7). With the gender-contrast prior on log M-ratio narrowed to SD 0.2 or widened to 1.0, the domain-general contrast in the discovery sample was +0.068 [0.011, 0.123] (BF10 = 4.3) and +0.080 [0.021, 0.139] (BF10 = 1.7), and in the replication BF10 was 0.38 and 0.09; the HDI excluded zero at every width in the discovery sample and included it at every width in the replication, with the posterior mean positive throughout. The meta-Δ contrast had BF10 above 10⁴ at every width in both samples. In the behavioural models, halving or doubling the contrast prior moved the gender contrasts by up to 1.9 pp for calibration and 0.06 scale points for confidence, and the domain-general gender effects on calibration and confidence remained decisive at every width. Bayes factors for smaller effects and for claims of no difference were less stable. With the narrower prior, which places more weight near zero, most claims of no difference fell below BF01 = 3; for example, BF01 for the gender difference in error slowing fell from 4.6 to 2.2, and for the replication gender × accuracy interaction from 5.6 to 2.7. Three small per-domain effects became inconclusive at one of the alternative widths: calibration in Calories (BF10 = 2.3 narrow, 2.4 wide), confidence in vision (BF10 = 2.4 wide) and mean reaction time in GDP (BF10 = 1.8 wide). The discovery-sample gender × accuracy interaction on confidence ranged from BF10 = 3.6 (narrow) to 1.2 (wide), which we treat as inconclusive. The claims of no difference in Supplementary Table S12 therefore rest on moderate evidence at the primary prior and should not be read as conclusive.
